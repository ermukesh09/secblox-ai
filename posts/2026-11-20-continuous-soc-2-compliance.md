**Continuous SOC 2 compliance means keeping your security controls running and producing evidence all year, so every audit simply confirms what already happens.** SOC 2 Type II tests whether controls operated over a period — so the work doesn't end when the first report arrives. Companies that treat SOC 2 as a calendar, not a project, find renewals routine; companies that stop after the first report usually start over.

## Why does SOC 2 need to be continuous?

A SOC 2 Type II report covers a specific period. Once it ends, customers start asking for the next one, and the next audit samples evidence from the whole new period. If access reviews, scans or backup tests stopped for a quarter, the auditor will find the gap — and it appears as an exception in the report your customers read.

Continuity also protects revenue. Enterprise buyers re-check vendors every year, and a lapsed or late report can stall a renewal or a new deal.

## How does SOC 2 renewal work?

After your first Type II report, most companies move to an annual cycle:

- **Back-to-back periods.** Each new observation period starts the day after the previous one ends, usually running 12 months, so coverage never has a gap.
- **Annual fieldwork.** The auditor tests the new period, typically shortly after it closes.
- **Bridge letters.** Between the end of a period and the new report, management issues a bridge letter confirming no material changes, so customers stay covered.
- **Scope updates.** New products, cloud accounts, vendors or AI features need to be added before the period starts, not discovered during fieldwork.

Many startups use a short first Type II period — often three to six months — to get a report to buyers sooner, then move to 12-month periods from the second audit onwards.

## What does a year-round SOC 2 control calendar look like?

Your own policies set the exact frequencies, but a typical calendar for a SaaS company looks like this:

| Frequency | Controls and evidence |
|---|---|
| Continuous | Logging and alerting, vulnerability scanning, change approvals, onboarding and offboarding |
| Monthly | Review security alerts and open vulnerabilities; check backups completed |
| Quarterly | User access reviews; review vendor and subprocessor changes; backup restore test |
| Annually | Risk assessment; policy review and acknowledgement; security awareness training; penetration test; vendor risk reviews; incident response tabletop; business continuity and disaster recovery test |
| Every change | Update the system description when infrastructure, products or key vendors change |

Put every row on a shared calendar with a named owner. If a task has no owner, it will be missed.

## What does a SOC 2 year look like, quarter by quarter?

| Quarter | Focus |
|---|---|
| Q1 — right after the report | Review the report and any exceptions; fix root causes; update the system description; confirm the next period's dates with your auditor |
| Q2 | Run controls on schedule; complete the first quarterly access review; refresh policies and training |
| Q3 | Mid-year check: run the risk assessment, penetration test and vendor reviews; test backups and disaster recovery |
| Q4 — before the period closes | Pre-audit review of all evidence; close gaps; prepare walkthroughs and the request list |

Spreading annual tasks across the year avoids the end-of-period scramble that causes most renewal exceptions.

## What changes in the second SOC 2 audit?

- **Prior exceptions get follow-up.** The auditor checks whether last year's exceptions were fixed and stayed fixed.
- **The full period is sampled.** With a 12-month window, sample sizes cover a whole year of onboarding, offboarding and changes.
- **"What changed?" matters most.** New systems, vendors, people and AI features are where gaps appear. Review changes every quarter, not at fieldwork.
- **Keeping the same auditor helps.** A firm that knows your environment usually runs a faster renewal; switch only if service, price or buyer acceptance becomes a problem. See [how to choose a SOC 2 auditor](/blog/how-to-choose-a-soc-2-auditor/).

## How do you keep evidence flowing without extra work?

- **Automate collection** for anything a system can prove — configurations, logs, MFA status, ticket histories.
- **Use tickets for recurring tasks** so every access review or restore test leaves a dated record.
- **Store evidence in one place**, organised by control, as it's created — not reconstructed the week before fieldwork.
- **Check monthly, not yearly.** A 30-minute monthly review catches a missed task while it can still be fixed.

A right-sized compliance tool helps here, but the calendar and the owners matter more than the software. See [SOC 2 consultant vs compliance platform](/blog/soc-2-consultant-vs-compliance-platform/).

## What breaks continuous compliance?

1. **The first report feels like the finish line** and the project team disbands.
2. **The control owner leaves** and nobody inherits their recurring tasks.
3. **The environment changes** — a new cloud account, product or AI vendor — without updating scope.
4. **Evidence lives in personal inboxes** and disappears with the person.
5. **Annual tasks bunch up** in the last month before fieldwork.

## In practice

The companies that find SOC 2 easy are the ones that keep it running all year. When access reviews, scans and evidence collection happen on schedule, the renewal audit is close to a formality. When they stop after the first report, every renewal becomes a new project — and often a second round of remediation. The difference is rarely tooling; it's a calendar, named owners and a short monthly check.

## FAQ

### How often do you need a SOC 2 audit?

There's no fixed legal requirement, but most companies renew annually because customers expect a report that covers the recent past. Back-to-back 12-month periods are the common pattern.

### Does a SOC 2 report expire after 12 months?

There's no official expiry date, but customers generally expect a report covering the recent past, so in practice a report is treated as current for about a year after its period ends. That's why renewals run annually with no gap between periods.

### What is a SOC 2 bridge letter?

A letter from management covering the gap between the end of your last SOC 2 period and today, confirming no material changes to controls, so customers have assurance until the next report.

### Is the second SOC 2 audit easier than the first?

It should be, because readiness and remediation are done. It's only easier if controls kept operating during the new period; otherwise the auditor finds exceptions across the year.

### Can we switch from Type I to Type II at renewal?

Yes. Many startups start with a Type I and move to a Type II period afterwards. See [SOC 2 Type I vs Type II](/blog/soc-2-type-1-vs-type-2/).
